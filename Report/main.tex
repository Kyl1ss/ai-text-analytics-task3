\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This project analyses the changes of machine learning research over time using the abstract of arXiv \texttt{cs.LG} from 1999 to 2021 for \textit{task 3: comparative corpus analysis}. After pre-processing 7,014 abstracts, we divide the corpus into four periods: \textit{early}, \textit{mid}, \textit{growth period} and \textit{recent}. We compared multiple text analysis methods, including TF-IDF, Word2Vec, Sentence-BERT, LDA topic modelling, classifier benchmarking and PCA-based time drift analysis. The results show that the vocabulary and research topics have changed significantly. The early stage is more related to probability theory, tree-based methods and optimisation-related methods, while the later stage is more closely related to deep learning, graph learning, federated learning and self-supervised learning. In period prediction, Word2Vec achieves the same accuracy rate as TF-IDF, but the macro F1 score is higher, which indicates that intensive semantic representation is more stable for unbalanced time series data. In general, the research results show that the combination of interpretable vocabulary methods, theme models, semantic embedding and drift visualisation can more reliably show the picture of machine learning research evolving over time.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

This report is aimed at \textit{Task 3: Comparative corpus Analysis}. The task aims to use the arXiv abstract corpus to analyse the changes of machine learning research over time. We do not regard the abstract as an isolated document, but use the year of its publication to compare different historical periods and identify whether the language, theme and semantic structure of machine learning research show obvious time changes.

The motivation of this task comes from the rapid development of machine learning as a research field. Machine learning has expanded from early statistical and optimisation methods to more recent fields, such as deep learning, graph learning, federated learning and self-supervised learning. Previous literature metrology and thematic modelling studies have shown that text-based analysis can be used to identify research trends and thematic evolution in the fields of artificial intelligence and machine learning \cite{li2020,su2021,yu2023}. This project follows this direction, but especially focusses on the arXiv \texttt{cs.LG} abstract, and compares a variety of text analysis methods used to detect time drift.

The available data consists of papers under the \texttt{cs.LG} classification related to machine learning research in arXiv. Each record contains the thesis ID, title, abstract, classification information and year of publication. After screening and cleaning, the data set contains 7,014 abstracts, covering the period from 1999 to 2021. Due to the significant difference in the number of papers in each year, we divide the corpus into four broader periods: \textit{early} (1999–2009), \textit{mid-term} (2010–2014), \textit{growth period} (2015–2017) and \textit{recent} (2018–2021). This makes the comparison results more stable and allows us to focus on long-term changes rather than year-to-year fluctuations.

Since the goal is to conduct a comparative analysis of the corpus, an excellent solution not only needs to produce a high prediction score system, but also provide explainable evidence of how the corpus changes. It should be able to recognise vocabulary in a specific period, broader thematic changes, and semantic evolution between different periods. In addition, since the proportion of recent papers in the corpus is much higher than that of early papers, the system also needs to deal with the imbalance in the data set. Finally, the analysis results are visualised in charts, and the changes are observed in an intuitive and transparent way.

The challenge of this task comes from the special development of machine learning research. Earlier methods may still appear in recent papers, while newer methods are often based on earlier terms. The abstract is usually short and may not fully present the technical details in the paper. Moreover, terms such as "learning", "model" and "data" appear widely in almost all periods, which makes it difficult to identify the uniqueness of trends. These problems are closely related to the broader problem of analysing the evolution of language and semantics over time \cite{frermann2016,tang2018}.

In order to complete task 3, we compared a variety of methods to capture different dimensions of time changes. Use TF-IDF as an interpretable baseline method to identify words with the characteristics of the times. Word2Vec is used to capture the semantic relationship between terms \cite{mikolov2013efficient,mikolov2013distributed} based on contextual similarity. Sentence-BERT is used to express the entire abstract as an intensive semantic embedding in \cite{reimers2019}. LDA topic modelling is used to identify a wider range of potential themes in the corpus \cite{blei2012}. Then use PCA to visualise time drift in low-dimensional space and track the movement of the centre of mass in various periods \cite{jolliffe2016}.

In general, this analysis aims to answer three main questions: which terms are most representative in different periods; whether the main research topics change with time; and whether different text representations show a consistent time drift direction.


\section{Methods}
% Around 3.5 pages

\subsection{Data Preprocessing and Dataset Preparation}

The arXiv abstracts from the \texttt{cs.LG} category, which deals with machine learning research, comprise the dataset for this project. We have adequate time to examine how the field has evolved because the publications span from 1999 to 2021. Paper ID, title, abstract, categories, and publication year were the five primary columns in the original filtered file, which had 7,015 items. After eliminating one duplicate abstract, we were left with 7,014 papers. We also looked for missing values in the important columns, but we couldn't locate any.

The abstract text was then cleaned. The text was tokenized using NLTK, all words were lowercased, unnecessary white space and punctuation were eliminated, and hyphenated terms were divided. Additionally, we eliminated English stopwords because terms like "the" and "of" don't actually convey anything about the subject of a paper. The old abstract was left in place so we could refer to it later if necessary, and the cleaned text was stored in a new \texttt{cleaned\_abstract} column. Additionally, we included two straightforward features for investigation: the number of words and characters in each abstract.

We did not work year by year because the annual amount of papers is so inconsistent. Rather, we divided the papers into four time periods:
Early (1999–2009), Mid (2010–2014), Growth (2015–2017), and Recent (2018–2021). There are 150, 724, 1,488, and 4,652 papers in each of the four periods, demonstrating the rapid growth of machine learning on arXiv. The majority of the dataset is from recent years. Since each bucket contains enough papers to be steady, grouping years into periods makes it easier to compare the trends.

We divided the data into three categories for modeling: 70\% train, 15\% validation, and 15\% test. To ensure that each split maintains the same balance of periods as the entire dataset, we employed stratified sampling on the period label. This provided us with 1,052 validation, 1,053 test examples, and 4,909 training. The cleansed dataset, the three divides, and a different file for each time were then saved so the pipeline could utilize them again.

\subsection{Text Representation and Baseline System}

The abstracts were preprocessed and then transformed into numerical representations for further text analysis. Three representations—TF-IDF, Word2Vec, and Sentence-BERT—were compared. These techniques were selected because they capture several levels of textual information, ranging from semantic similarity to explicit vocabulary patterns.

The baseline representation was TF-IDF. It treats each abstract as a sparse vector of word significance scores, giving more weight to terms that appear often in one document but are less common across the corpus. Because of this, it can be used to detect unique terminology from different eras. Since many machine learning concepts, like \textit{deep learning}, \textit{support vector}, and \textit{reinforcement learning}, are stated as multi-word phrases, we employed both unigrams and bigrams with an n-gram range of $(1,2)$. Only alphabetic tokens with three or more letters were kept after English stopwords were eliminated.

We also fitted separate TF-IDF models for the four periods: \textit{early}, \textit{mid}, \textit{growth}, and \textit{recent}. This allowed us to identify period-specific terms and make vocabulary shifts more interpretable. In addition, a count matrix was created using the same vocabulary settings. Unlike TF-IDF, the count matrix stores raw word frequencies and was used for topic modelling methods such as LDA, which require count-based inputs.

The second representation was Word2Vec. While TF-IDF is based on word frequency, Word2Vec learns dense word embeddings from context, placing words with similar usage closer in vector space. The model was trained on the cleaned arXiv abstracts to capture domain-specific semantic relationships. Each abstract was represented by averaging the vectors of its words, producing a 100-dimensional document vector. We also inspected similar words for key machine learning terms to check whether the embeddings captured meaningful relationships within the corpus.

The third representation was Sentence-BERT. We used the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model to encode each abstract as a 384-dimensional sentence embedding. Unlike Word2Vec, which builds document vectors from individual word embeddings, Sentence-BERT directly represents the overall meaning of the abstract. This is useful for comparing papers that discuss similar research problems using different terminology.

Overall, the three representations serve different roles. TF-IDF provides interpretable lexical evidence, Word2Vec captures domain-level semantic relationships, and Sentence-BERT provides document-level semantic representations. The resulting TF-IDF matrix, count matrix, Word2Vec embeddings, and Sentence-BERT embeddings were saved for later comparison, topic analysis, dimensionality reduction, and evaluation.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis Methods}

In order to analyse the changes of machine learning research over time, we have designed a comparative analysis framework, focussing on the time drift phenomenon in the arXiv abstract corpus. Instead of relying on a single method, we compared three complementary methods: keyword-based analysis, LDA topic modelling and classifier benchmarking. This enables us to examine time changes from different angles, including unique vocabulary, potential theme structures, and the ability of different text representation methods to distinguish between research periods.

The first method is to analyse keywords. Instead of using simple word frequency statistics, we use a logistic  regression classifier that has been trained to predict the research period to which each abstract belongs. This classifier generates characteristic importance fractions as statistics. logistic  regression will allocate coefficients to input characteristics, so terms with large positive coefficients can be interpreted as more distinguishing indicators for a specific period. This method was chosen because it helps to filter out common machine learning words that appear in all periods, while highlighting terms that are more closely associated with specific historical stages. We assume that this method can identify clear technical signs in different periods, such as Bayesian and tree-based terms that appear in the early stage, support vector machines and optimisation terms in the middle stage, and deep learning or diagram-related terms in the later stage.

The second method is LDA topic modelling. We apply LDA to the counting-based document-vocabulary matrix to identify five potential topics in the corpus. Each abstract is represented by the distribution of these topics, and then the average topic weight for each period (early, medium, growing and recent) is calculated. This method is adopted because keyword analysis can only capture a single term, while topic modelling can reveal a wider range of research topics expressed through related phrases. We assume that LDA will show the changes at the macro level of machine learning research structure, from traditional statistical learning and optimisation to neural networks, deep learning and graph-based methods.

The third method is the classifier benchmark test. For the task of predicting the period to which each abstract belongs, we compared the traditional TF-IDF representation with the intensive representation based on Word2Vec. This itself is not intended as a practical application task, but indirectly evaluates the degree of distinction between different periods through language and semantic content. If the classifier can predict the period of abstract affiliation with reasonable performance, it indicates that the vocabulary and semantic structure of machine learning research have changed over time. The comparison between TF-IDF and Word2Vec is reasonable because they represent the text in different ways. TF-IDF captures sparse and interpretable lexical patterns, while Word2Vec captures the dense semantic relationship between words. Our hypothesis is that for a few periods, Word2Vec will be more robust, because even if the exact vocabulary overlap is limited, it can group semantically related terms.

These three methods together constitute a comparative framework for analysing time drift. Keyword analysis provides explainable specific period terms, LDA reveals broader changes at the subject level, and classifier benchmarks evaluate whether different representation methods can distinguish different research periods. The comprehensive application of these methods enhances the reliability of the analysis, so that the research results can not only rely on a single model, but can be compared based on multiple types of evidence.

\subsection{Dimensionality Reduction and Temporal Drift Analysis}

All three text representations described earlier (TF-IDF, Word2Vec and Sentence-BERT) live in quite high dimensional spaces: approximately 50,000, 100 and 384 dimensions, respectively. We applied PCA to each representation in order to see how machine learning research has shifted across the four periods and to get something we can actually plot. We chose PCA because it is simple, deterministic, and the components are ordered by the amount of explained variance. This makes it easier to compare results across time and across representations than say tSNE or UMAP where the output can change a lot depending on random seed and the chosen parameters.

\paragraph{Preparing each representation.}
PCA is sensitive to feature scale, so each representation needed a small amount of preparation. For Word2Vec and Sentence-BERT, which produce dense vectors, we mean-centred each feature and scaled it to unit variance. The mean and standard deviation were computed on the training set only and then applied to the validation and test sets, to avoid leaking information between splits. TF-IDF is sparse and very high-dimensional, so running standard PCA on it directly was not practical. We used TruncatedSVD instead, which is the usual stand-in for PCA on sparse matrices. TruncatedSVD does not centre the data, but our TF-IDF vectors are already L2-normalised, so the components are still comparable across documents. For simplicity we just call this step PCA throughout the report.

\paragraph{How many components.}
We used two different numbers of components depending on what we wanted to do with them. For visualisation we kept only two components, so that documents could be plotted directly with their period as a colour. For the quantitative drift analysis we kept 50 components, which gives more stable centroid estimates because more of the variance is preserved. We also report the cumulative explained variance for each representation so it is clear how much of the signal these projections actually keep.

\paragraph{Tracking drift with period centroids.}
To get a number for how much the corpus moves over time, we took the mean of all training documents in each period within the reduced space. This gives one centroid per period. Following these centroids in order (\textit{early} $\rightarrow$ \textit{mid} $\rightarrow$ \textit{growth} $\rightarrow$ \textit{recent}) traces out a path that shows the overall direction of change. We measured this in two ways. The first is the Euclidean distance between consecutive centroids, which tells us how much the average paper changes from one period to the next. The second is the distance from the \textit{early} centroid to each later one, which tells us how far things have moved overall. Because the periods have very different sizes, raw centroids could be biased toward the bigger periods, so we also recomputed the distances on bootstrap subsamples of equal size and reported the resulting confidence intervals.

\paragraph{Verification of the reality of the drift.}
One concern we had is whether the drift we see is really about the field changing, or just an artifact of a particular representation. To verify this, we performed the same PCA-based analysis on all three representations and compared the results. We were looking at two things. First, the order of the four period centroids along the main components: if drift is real, the chronological order should be roughly maintained across representations. Second, we measured the Spearman rank correlation between the magnitudes of the per-period drifts of each pair of representations. This gives us more confidence that the change is in the research itself, and not in how we encoded the text, when TF-IDF, Word2Vec and Sentence-BERT all agree.

\paragraph{What this stage produces.}

This part of the pipeline gives us three kinds of plots for the results section.
The first plot is a 2D PCA scatter plot of the corpus, coloured by period. It helps us see how the papers move over time at a glance.
The second plot shows where the four period centroids are, in the 2D space. Arrows connect periods so we can easily see the direction of change.
The third plot is a bar chart that shows how apart consecutive periods are. It also includes bootstrap confidence intervals giving us a number to go with the visual.
The plots help us understand the corpus and period changes. The PCA scatter plot shows the corpus distribution. The trajectory plot shows period centroid movement. The bar chart shows period distance changes.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Because this project combines classification, topic analysis, and temporal drift analysis, we used different evaluation measures for different parts of the pipeline. For the classifier benchmarking task, we reported accuracy and macro F1-score. Accuracy gives an intuitive measure of overall performance, but it is limited in this dataset because the four periods are highly imbalanced. The \textit{recent} period contains far more papers than the \textit{early} period, so a model may achieve reasonable accuracy by mainly predicting the majority period. Macro F1-score is therefore also reported, because it gives equal weight to each period and better reflects performance on smaller classes.

For keyword analysis and LDA topic modelling, the main evaluation was interpretability. For keyword analysis, we inspected the terms with the highest logistic regression coefficients and checked whether they made sense as period-specific technical markers. For LDA, we inspected the top words in each topic and the average topic weights across periods. These evaluations are partly qualitative, but they are suitable for our aim because the project is mainly about understanding research change rather than only maximising predictive accuracy.

For PCA-based temporal drift analysis, we used both visual and distance-based measures. The 2D PCA plots show whether papers from different periods move in the reduced space, while centroid trajectories show the overall direction of movement from \textit{early} to \textit{recent}. Euclidean distances between period centroids provide a numerical summary of drift. However, PCA only preserves part of the original high-dimensional information, so these plots should be interpreted as simplified views of the corpus.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

The preprocessed dataset and period labels mentioned in the Methods section were utilized. A 70/15/15 split with stratification by period was used to divide the data into training, validation, and test sets. The test set was set aside for final evaluation, the validation set was utilized to verify model behavior and parameter selections, and the training set was used to fit the primary models and text representations.

For TF-IDF, we used unigram and bigram features with an n-gram range of $(1,2)$, removed English stopwords, and kept alphabetic tokens with at least three letters. The same tokenisation settings were used to create the count matrix for LDA, so that the TF-IDF and topic modelling results were based on comparable preprocessing. LDA was fitted with five topics, and the average topic distribution was calculated for each period.

For Word2Vec, the model was trained on the cleaned abstracts, and each document was represented by averaging its word vectors. Sentence-BERT used the \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2} model to encode each abstract into a 384-dimensional sentence embedding \cite{reimers2019}. Logistic regression classifiers were then trained to predict the period label, and their coefficients were also used to identify period-specific keywords.

For dimensionality reduction, PCA was applied to the standardised Word2Vec and Sentence-BERT embeddings. For TF-IDF, TruncatedSVD was used as the sparse equivalent of PCA. Two components were used for visualisation, while 50 components were used for quantitative drift measurement. Period centroids were calculated in the reduced space, and drift was measured using distances between consecutive centroids. Bootstrap subsampling with equal period sizes was used to reduce the effect of period imbalance.

The dataset is strongly imbalanced across time. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:data_overview}, the number of papers increases sharply in later years, and the \textit{recent} period contains most of the abstracts. This supports our decision to group years into broader periods and also shows why accuracy should be interpreted carefully.

Deep learning terms like \textit{deep}, \textit{rnn}, and \textit{networks} have become more popular during the \textit{growth} period. \subsection{Error Analysis}

The main errors in the classifier benchmark come from overlap between adjacent periods. Machine learning research does not change suddenly from one topic to another. Older methods continue to appear in later papers, and newer methods often build on older terminology. As a result, the classifier sometimes struggles to draw clear boundaries between periods.

One common source of confusion is between the \textit{early} and \textit{mid} periods. Both contain traditional machine learning language, including probabilistic modelling, optimisation, kernels, and classical supervised learning methods. This is made harder by the small size of the \textit{early} period, which gives the model fewer examples to learn from.

Another source of confusion is between the \textit{growth} and \textit{recent} periods. Deep learning terms became prominent in the growth period but remained common in recent papers. Therefore, words such as \textit{deep}, \textit{neural}, and \textit{network} are not always enough to distinguish the two periods. Recent papers may include newer markers such as \textit{graph}, \textit{federated}, or \textit{self-supervised}, but these do not appear in every recent abstract.

There are also general domain terms, such as \textit{learning}, \textit{model}, \textit{data}, and \textit{algorithm}, that appear across the whole corpus. These words reduce the distinctiveness of LDA topics and can make keyword analysis less precise. This suggests that a more specialised machine learning stopword list could improve the analysis.

\subsection{Discussion}

Overall, the findings point to a significant evolution in machine learning research across time, but one that is more gradual than clearly divided into discrete eras. The uniformity across several approaches provides the most compelling proof. A similar pattern can be seen in keyword analysis, LDA topic modeling, classifier benchmarking, and PCA-based centroid movement: the field shifts from traditional statistical and optimization-based techniques to deep learning, followed by more modern themes like graph learning, federated learning, and self-supervised learning.

Different types of evidence were given by each method. TF-IDF was helpful in detecting period-specific language because to its simplicity and good interpretability. It may overlook terms that are semantically related, though, depending on the precise word overlap. Although Word2Vec's higher macro F1-score indicates that dense semantic representations are more resilient for shorter durations, it was less directly interpretable. Sentence-BERT was helpful for drift visualization and offered deeper document-level semantic information, but it is more difficult to pinpoint the precise words that cause the change. Although LDA provided a more comprehensive topic-level perspective, some topics' individuality was diminished by the use of generic machine learning terms.

The key finding is that temporal change in machine learning research cannot be adequately explained by a single approach. LDA displays topic-level movement, PCA provides a visual and numerical summary of drift, Word2Vec and Sentence-BERT offer semantic views, and TF-IDF provides more lucid vocabulary evidence. When combined, they provide a more trustworthy image than when used separately. By creating a more specialized machine learning stopword list, experimenting with varying LDA topic counts, and employing temporal topic models that explicitly capture change over time, future research could enhance the study.


\section{Conclusion}

Using the abstract under the \texttt{cs.LG} classification in arXiv, we analysed the evolution of machine learning research over time. Clear evidence that terms and research topics drift over time can be found by dividing the literature into four periods and comparing a variety of text analysis techniques. The results show that emerging fields such as deep learning and graph learning, joint learning and self-supervised learning have replaced the early methods based on probability, tree structure and optimisation.

We have used the strengths of different methods. Because the TF-IDF method is intuitive and effective, it is very useful as a benchmark method. It makes the changes in terms very obvious and can identify the linguistic characteristics of a specific period. The comparison depends on the word frequency alone, and the logical regression coefficient helps to extract the unique keywords of each period. LDA theme modelling reveals more common changes in research topics, including the emergence of topics related to graph theory and neural networks in the later stage. In terms of macro F1 scores predicted by the period, Word2Vec's performance is better than TF-IDF, showing that intensive semantic representation is more robust when precise keyword overlap is limited. The method of quantitative and qualitative summary of the overall trend of time drift based on the centre of mass analysis of PCA is also remarkable.

Some approaches, meanwhile, had obvious drawbacks or were less successful. Because TF-IDF heavily relies on precise word overlap, it could overlook situations in which equivalent concepts are expressed using different words. Broad domain words like \textit{learning}, \textit{model}, and \textit{data} occasionally had an impact on LDA topics, decreasing topic distinctiveness. Richer semantic representations were offered by Word2Vec and Sentence-BERT, but they were less transparent than TF-IDF, making it more difficult to pinpoint the precise words that brought about the observed alterations. The findings of the classifier also demonstrated that the periods cannot be clearly distinguished. This is to be expected given the steady evolution of study disciplines and the persistence of earlier techniques in subsequent publications.

The difficulty of accurately measuring the change of time in the study has become the main challenge at present. The number of papers published recently in the arXiv corpus is much more than in the early days, and the time imbalance is significant. Therefore, the mere accuracy rate may be misleading, because the arXiv abstract only reflects part of the machine learning research, and the research results should be regarded as a trend within the \texttt{cs.LG} arXiv corpus, not the trend of the whole field. Future research is expected to be further improved by using more professional machine learning inactive word lists, trying different numbers of LDA themes, and implementing timing theme models that can clearly explain the changes of topics over time.

In general, multi-methodal integration can indeed better present the evolution of machine learning research. Semantic embedding reveals deep similarities, thematic modelling captures general research topics, keyword analysis provides explainable evidence, and PCA visualises the shift in the research direction. Overall, these findings show that in addition to the increase in the number of publications, the language and topics in the research abstract have also undergone systematic changes.
